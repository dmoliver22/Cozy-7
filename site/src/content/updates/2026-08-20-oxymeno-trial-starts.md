## What happened

On 20 August 2026, University Hospital Basel began enrolling women in OxyMENO (NCT07742124), a study comparing oxytocin levels in postmenopausal women with premenopausal controls. The primary hypothesis is that cumulative neurophysin-I, measured as the area under the curve over one month, is lower after menopause. Primary completion is estimated for June 2027, with the full study wrapping up around September 2027 [1][2].

It is, as far as we can find, the newest registered oxytocin trial anywhere, and the first to ask directly whether menopause lowers oxytocin.

## What neurophysin-I is, and why it matters

Oxytocin itself is nearly impossible to measure well. It disappears from the blood within minutes, and different lab kits give wildly different numbers for the same sample. Endocrinologists say no validated routine test exists [6].

Neurophysin-I is the workaround. The body makes oxytocin from a larger precursor protein, and when that precursor is cut, it releases one oxytocin molecule and one neurophysin-I molecule together. Neurophysin-I lasts much longer in the blood and circulates at higher levels, so it can be measured with a standard sandwich immunoassay [5]. A 2024 University of Arizona study validated it as an "analytically robust surrogate biomarker" for oxytocin [4]. In Basel's own work, stimulated neurophysin-I separated oxytocin-deficient patients from healthy controls perfectly in a conference abstract [5].

Measuring neurophysin-I over a month, rather than oxytocin in a single draw, is what makes OxyMENO's question answerable at all.

## Why menopause and oxytocin deficiency now share a storyline

The same Basel group, led by Mirjam Christ-Crain, showed in 2023 that people with arginine vasopressin deficiency, a pituitary disorder, have a true oxytocin deficiency. Using MDMA as a stimulation test, oxytocin rose about 8.5-fold in healthy volunteers and did not budge in patients [3]. That established oxytocin deficiency as a real clinical state, at least in pituitary disease.

Menopause is the obvious next question. Estrogen and oxytocin are tightly linked: estrogen boosts oxytocin production and receptor expression, and Basel's sister trial PHOENIX is testing oral estrogens as a safer oxytocin stimulation test. If oxytocin falls when estrogen falls, some menopause symptoms that estrogen therapy helps, including low mood, detachment and low desire, might partly run through oxytocin.

Consumer menopause writing already says this, usually as "losing your moxy is down to oxy." OxyMENO is the first attempt to check it with a reliable marker.

One caution. Even if neurophysin-I is lower after menopause, that does not show that oxytocin replacement would help, or that nasal spray is the way to replace it. The one oxytocin product with menopause trial data, a vaginal gel for atrophy, improved clinically assessed atrophy in a 2023 pooled analysis of 7 studies and 631 women but did not significantly improve painful intercourse, vaginal pH or tissue samples [7]. A Phase 3 trial of the gel is now enrolling.

The treatments with real evidence, hormone therapy for those who can use it and the non-hormonal options for those who cannot, remain the place to start.
