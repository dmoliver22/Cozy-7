## What happened

On 15 July 2026, Alcohol: Clinical and Experimental Research published the results of the largest controlled trial of nasal oxytocin for alcohol use disorder. Nabil Tiouririne and colleagues randomized 100 adults with alcohol use disorder to oxytocin or placebo spray for 12 weeks. The oxytocin arm took 35 IU twice a day, up to 70 IU daily, which is at the high end of doses used in any oxytocin trial [1].

The trial was a multisite effort sponsored through the National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism, with sites at the University of California Los Angeles, Johns Hopkins, Boston Medical Center and the University of Virginia [1].

The primary result: no difference between oxytocin and placebo in the percentage of heavy drinking days [1].

Two secondary findings are worth noting. People on oxytocin scored lower on measures of anger and physical aggression than people on placebo. And the most common side effect of oxytocin was a reduced sense of smell, which makes sense for a drug sprayed up the nose twice a day for three months [1].

## What it means

This is the answer the field has been waiting for since animal studies in the 2010s suggested oxytocin might reduce drinking and withdrawal. A 12-week trial in 100 people at a high dose is a fair test. Oxytocin did not help people drink less.

It fits the wider pattern. A 2026 meta-analysis of all alcohol trials, using a Bayesian approach, concluded that nasal oxytocin "does not confer a consistent therapeutic benefit across alcohol-related outcomes," with only context-dependent signals in social and cognitive measures [2]. And in 2025, adding 40 IU of oxytocin to couples therapy for drinking in 96 couples produced no benefit beyond therapy alone [3].

The anger and aggression result is interesting and should be tested on its own. It is not a reason to take oxytocin for drinking. Lower anger scores on a questionnaire did not translate into fewer heavy drinking days, which was the point of the trial.

The smell finding is a small but real cost. Most oxytocin trials last days or weeks. This one ran three months at 70 IU a day and found the nose does not like it. Anyone using a compounded spray daily for months should know that.

## What we changed on the site

Our page on [oxytocin for alcohol and addiction](/oxytocin-for/alcohol-and-addiction/) now leads with this trial and carries the verdict "Tested, didn't work" for alcohol use disorder. The page separates alcohol, where the evidence is now negative, from opioid and benzodiazepine withdrawal, where only small pilot studies exist and the question is still open.

The page also lists what does work for alcohol use disorder: approved medications, structured therapy, and support programs with decades of evidence. If drinking is the problem, those are where to start, with a doctor who knows your history.
