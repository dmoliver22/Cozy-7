## What happened

In August 2026, the journal Alpha Psychiatry published a meta-analysis pooling every randomized controlled trial of oxytocin for social functioning in autism. The authors found 12 trials with 733 participants in total. Pooling them with a random-effects model, they found no statistically significant effect of oxytocin on social outcomes [1].

The exact publication date was not given in the sources we could reach; we are dating this update to mid-August.

## What it means

This is confirmation, not news. The question was largely settled in 2021, when Linmarie Sikich and colleagues published the largest single trial in the New England Journal of Medicine: 290 children and adolescents with autism, 24 weeks of daily nasal oxytocin or placebo. There was no benefit on social withdrawal or any secondary measure [2].

What the new meta-analysis adds is breadth. Twelve trials from different labs, countries and dosing schedules, combined, still show nothing. That rules out the argument that one big negative trial got the dose or the age range wrong.

One wrinkle. A separate 2026 meta-analysis from a Turkish group pooled 54 studies and 2,593 cases and reported a significant reduction in autism symptoms with oxytocin [3]. That analysis mixed animal studies with human trials, which is why it reaches a different conclusion. Animal data on oxytocin and social behavior have always been positive. Human trials have not. When you count only randomized trials in people, as the Alpha Psychiatry analysis does, the effect disappears.

The history here matters for parents. In the early 2010s, small studies showing oxytocin improved eye contact or emotion recognition got wide coverage. Some doctors began prescribing it off-label, and some parents bought products online, to the point that researchers worried off-label use was becoming widespread [4]. Then the properly sized trials arrived and found nothing. Compounded oxytocin is still marketed for "autism spectrum support" by some pharmacies. The evidence does not support it.

## What we changed on the site

Our page on [oxytocin for autism](/oxytocin-for/autism/) carries the verdict "Tested, didn't work." It now cites this meta-analysis alongside the 2021 trial, explains why the two 2026 meta-analyses disagree, and lists the interventions with real evidence for autistic children and adults, including behavioral and developmental therapies, speech and occupational therapy, and support for co-occurring anxiety and sleep problems.

If you are a parent who tried oxytocin, you were following what the early science seemed to say. The science changed. Talk to a clinician who knows your child about where to put your energy now.
