## What happened

One year ago today, on 24 September 2025, Acadia Pharmaceuticals announced that COMPASS PWS, its Phase 3 trial of intranasal carbetocin for the relentless hunger of Prader-Willi syndrome, had failed. The trial enrolled 175 participants aged 5 to 30 and treated them for 12 weeks. Carbetocin did not beat placebo on the primary endpoint, change in the Hyperphagia Questionnaire for Clinical Trials at week 12, and showed no separation on any secondary endpoint. Safety was fine. Acadia discontinued the program [1][2].

The anniversary lands with the field's next test pushed back. Tonix Pharmaceuticals had planned to start a Phase 2 trial of TNX-2900, its magnesium-potentiated nasal oxytocin, in Prader-Willi during 2026. The company now says the first quarter of 2027 [3]. The design is unchanged: children and adolescents aged 8 to 17.5, randomized equally to one of three doses or placebo for 12 weeks [3][4]. Tonix has not said publicly why the start slipped.

## Why Prader-Willi was supposed to be the easy case

If oxytocin was ever going to work as a brain drug, Prader-Willi syndrome was the place. People with the condition have about 42 percent fewer oxytocin-producing neurons in the hypothalamus than people without it [6]. That is a measurable deficit in a defined population with a devastating, specific symptom. Replace what is missing, the logic went, and hunger should ease.

Carbetocin is a longer-lasting cousin of oxytocin that is already used by injection to prevent bleeding after cesarean delivery. Acadia ran the biggest, best-funded trial of an oxytocin-receptor drug for a brain indication ever attempted. It found nothing.

## The lesson for every other oxytocin drug

There are three ways to read the failure, and each has consequences.

First, the drug may not reach the target. Whether nasal sprays deliver meaningful oxytocin to the human brain is still unresolved. If carbetocin never got to the hunger circuits, no dose would have worked. That reading casts doubt on every nasal oxytocin program, including Tonix's.

Second, the dose or formulation may be wrong. This is Tonix's bet. The company argues that oxytocin shows "paradoxical high-dose inhibition," where more drug produces less effect, and that adding magnesium smooths the dose response and lets it use lower doses [5]. If that is right, carbetocin at a fixed dose could have overshot.

Third, oxytocin may simply not control hunger in Prader-Willi the way the neuron count suggests. The brain compensates for missing signals in ways animal models do not capture.

Nobody knows which reading is correct. That is why the TNX-2900 trial matters more than its size suggests. A positive result would rescue the second reading and keep nasal oxytocin alive as a drug class. A negative result, on top of carbetocin, would make the first or third reading hard to escape, and would weigh on every program that depends on nasal oxytocin reaching the brain, from tinnitus to couples therapy.

Tonix's guidance has since moved again, to the second half of 2027. With a 12-week treatment period in a rare disease, results are unlikely before late 2028.
