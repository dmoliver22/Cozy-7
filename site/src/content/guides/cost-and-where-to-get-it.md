A bottle of compounded oxytocin nasal spray costs a pharmacy customer somewhere between $21 and $120 [1]. The same bottle through an online telehealth subscription costs $99 to $206 every month [2][3]. The difference is not the drug. It is the prescription, the subscription, and the marketing wrapped around a cheap, off-patent hormone. This page lays out every price we could verify as of October 2026 and tells you what each tier actually includes.

## The price table

All prices are US, cash-pay, observed in October 2026. Pharmacy figures come from a price aggregator that collects quotes from compounding pharmacies; they are claimed ranges, not a surveyed market price, and no independent survey exists [1]. Telehealth figures come from the sellers' own pages.

|---|---|---|---|
| Nasal spray, pharmacy fill | $21 to $120 per fill, median about $60 | The bottle only. You bring your own prescription. | [1] |
| Troches (lozenges), pharmacy fill | $3 to $6.67 per troche; $10 to $200 per fill | Lozenges only. Quantity varies by prescription. | [4] |
| 90-day supply, some pharmacies | About $200 | Three months of spray. | [5] |

A note on the low tier. We have seen plans advertised from about $99, but the lowest price we could confirm on a live seller page was $115 [2]. Treat $99 as the floor of the range, not a quote.

A note on bottles. The standard compounded spray is a 10 mL bottle at 50 IU/mL or 100 IU/mL [10]. Two sellers can quote the same monthly price and ship you half as much drug, so ask for the concentration and volume in writing.

## What each tier actually includes

The pharmacy price buys the drug and nothing else. You need a prescriber willing to write for an off-label compounded hormone, and many will not.

The low telehealth tier buys access to a prescriber who will. The visit is short. Some are asynchronous, meaning a clinician reads your questionnaire and never speaks to you. Then a 503A compounding pharmacy makes the spray and ships it [2].

The mid and high tiers buy the same thing with more words around it: "prescribed and monitored," longer-term plans, and a higher concentration [6][3]. We could not find any seller describing what "monitoring" consists of beyond the ability to message a provider.

None of the tiers buys evidence. The best recent couples trial, 96 couples given 40 IU before each therapy session, found the spray well tolerated and no better than placebo for drinking or for the relationship [11]. No seller's price includes a study showing their product does what the page implies.

## Insurance

Expect to pay cash. Compounded oxytocin is prescribed for uses no regulator has approved, and every seller we reviewed quotes a flat monthly price with no insurance option [2][6][7][3]. If a page tells you insurance "may cover" compounded oxytocin for bonding or anxiety, ask them to name a plan that has.

## The products that pollute your search results

Type "oxytocin price" into a search engine and most of what comes back is not for you.

**Veterinary oxytocin.** Farm and pet retailers sell 100 mL vials of injectable oxytocin for horses, cattle and pigs at roughly $10 to $13 [12]. It is formulated at 20 USP units per mL and requires veterinary authorization [13]. This is the product people mean when they say "oxytocin is cheap." It is an injection made to animal standards. It is not sterile-tested for human use, it is not a nasal spray, and self-injecting it is dangerous. The 15-fold gap between a $10 vet vial and a $150 telehealth plan is real, and it is also irrelevant, because they are not substitutes.

**Obstetric oxytocin.** Pitocin and its generics are the FDA-approved injection used to induce labor and control bleeding after birth [14]. Hospital pricing for it shows up in search results and has nothing to do with what you would pay for a spray. It is also the only oxytocin product that is approved for anything.

**"Research use only" peptide.** Online vendors sell oxytocin powder in vials labeled "not for human consumption." The FDA has been sending warning letters to these sellers since 2025 and treats the label as a ruse when the marketing is aimed at people. We do not link to them and you should not buy from them.

**Pheromone and homeopathic sprays.** These are sold over the counter, often for $20 to $50, under names that include the word oxytocin. They are not oxytocin. We cover them on a separate page.

## What are you paying for?

This is the honest question, so here is the honest answer.

You are paying for a prescriber's willingness. Oxytocin is an old, cheap hormone. The bottle costs the pharmacy very little to make. What is scarce is a licensed clinician who will write for it off-label, and the telehealth companies have built a business on being that clinician at scale.

You are paying for convenience. A 15-minute online form and a box at your door is genuinely easier than persuading your own doctor.

You are paying for marketing. The copy on these pages talks about connection, intimacy and bonding. The trials behind those words are small lab studies from more than a decade ago, and the recent, larger trials have come back negative. The FDA has already sent roughly 130 warning letters since September 2025 to telehealth companies over how they describe compounded drugs, objecting to phrases like "clinically proven" and "FDA approved" [15]. That marketing is part of the price.

You are not paying for evidence that it works. Nobody has that to sell.

## Where this leaves you

If you have decided to try compounded oxytocin, the cheapest legitimate route is a prescriber you already see, sending the prescription to a licensed compounding pharmacy, so you pay something near the pharmacy fill price. If you use telehealth, compare concentration, bottle volume and commitment length, not just the monthly number, and know which pharmacy is making your spray. And before you spend $100 or $200 a month on a hormone with no approved use, read what the trials found for the problem you are actually trying to solve. For most of the reasons people buy it, the proven help costs less and works better.
