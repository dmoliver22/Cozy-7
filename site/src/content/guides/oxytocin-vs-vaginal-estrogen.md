For dryness, thinning and pain with sex after menopause, low-dose vaginal estrogen and vaginal DHEA are the proven treatments. They are approved, backed by decades of trials, and recommended first by menopause specialists for moderate to severe symptoms [1]. Vaginal oxytocin gel is a research idea. In 7 small trials with 631 women, it improved how the tissue looked to an examining clinician, but it did not significantly improve pain with sex, vaginal pH or tissue samples under the microscope [7]. The Phase 3 trial that could settle the question was listed as not yet enrolling at its last registry update [8]. No oxytocin gel is approved anywhere we could find.

## What goes wrong after menopause

When estrogen falls, the lining of the vagina gets thinner, drier and less stretchy, and its pH rises. Doctors call this genitourinary syndrome of menopause, or GSM. The older name was vaginal atrophy.

It is common and it does not get better on its own. The North American Menopause Society's 2020 position statement puts the share of postmenopausal women affected somewhere between 27 and 84 percent, depending on how it is measured [1]. Symptoms include dryness, burning, pain with sex (dyspareunia), urinary urgency and repeat urinary infections.

Both treatments in this comparison are trying to reverse the same thing: a tissue starved of the hormone that kept it healthy.

## The proven standard: vaginal estrogen and DHEA

Low-dose vaginal estrogen comes as a cream, a tablet, an insert or a ring. It restores the lining, lowers pH, improves lubrication and reduces pain with sex, and it has been studied in thousands of women over decades [1].

Because the dose is small and it acts where it is placed, blood estrogen levels stay in or near the postmenopausal range [1]. That is why the 2020 statement treats it as suitable for most women, including many who have been told to avoid hormone pills.

Vaginal DHEA (prasterone) is the second approved option. It is an insert that the vaginal tissue converts into small amounts of estrogen and testosterone locally. The FDA approved it in November 2016 for moderate to severe pain with sex due to menopause [3]. A third option, ospemifene, is a daily pill approved in 2013 for the same symptom; it acts like estrogen on vaginal tissue without being estrogen [4].

For mild symptoms, the statement recommends starting with non-hormonal lubricants for sex and long-acting vaginal moisturizers used regularly [1]. Many women need nothing more.

Plain-English read: this is settled medicine. The treatments work, the side effects are known, and a clinician trained in menopause care can match one to your history.

## The challenger: oxytocin gel

The idea behind oxytocin gel is reasonable. Vaginal tissue carries oxytocin receptors, and early lab work suggested oxytocin applied directly might encourage the lining to grow back without raising hormone levels in the blood.

The first controlled test was a small Swedish pilot published in 2011. Postmenopausal women used either an oxytocin gel or a placebo gel, double-blind, and the researchers reported that signs of atrophy reversed in the oxytocin group [5]. A later Swedish trial used a gel at 600 IU per mL and reported that it counteracted the physical signs of atrophy [6].

By 2023 there were enough trials to pool. The BMC Women's Health meta-analysis combined 7 randomized trials with 631 postmenopausal women, comparing vaginal oxytocin gel with placebo [7]. Three findings matter.

First, clinically assessed atrophy, meaning how the tissue looked and felt to the examining clinician, improved significantly with oxytocin [7].

Second, dyspareunia, the symptom women most want fixed, did not differ significantly from placebo [7].

Third, vaginal pH and histology, the tissue sample under a microscope, also did not differ significantly [7]. Those are the objective markers that confirm the lining has truly recovered, and estrogen moves both of them.

Plain-English read: the exam looked better, but the symptom did not clearly change and the objective markers did not move. The trials were small, used different gel strengths and lengths of treatment, and followed women for weeks, not years. The authors themselves were skeptical that oxytocin could stand in for estrogen on this evidence [7].

One more thing to keep in mind: a placebo gel is itself a moisturizer. Some of the improvement in both groups likely came from simply applying something soothing, which makes a small true effect harder to see.

## The Phase 3 that would settle it

NCT06514586 is registered as a Phase 3, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of topical vaginal oxytocin gel in postmenopausal women with vulvovaginal atrophy, with about 242 participants planned. The sponsor is listed as Oxagon [8].

Here is the problem. The registry entry was first posted on 23 July 2024 with a status of "not yet recruiting," an estimated start of September 2024 and an estimated completion of August 2025. It has not been updated since, and its overall status now reads as unknown [8]. Those dates have passed with no news, no published protocol and no company announcement we could find.

A Phase 3 that has not started cannot read out. Until it does, the oxytocin gel evidence is the 7 small trials and nothing more.

A separate Basel study, OxyMENO (NCT07742124), started in August 2026 and is measuring whether postmenopausal women have lower oxytocin-related markers than premenopausal women. It does not treat anyone, and its results are expected in 2027 [10]. If it finds a difference, that would strengthen the biological story for oxytocin in menopause. It would not make the gel work.

## Who might reasonably want a non-estrogen option

The honest audience for an oxytocin gel, if it ever proves itself, is women who cannot or will not use estrogen. The largest group is women with a history of breast cancer, especially estrogen-sensitive breast cancer, many of whom are on treatments that make GSM worse.

Two caveats matter here. First, the guidance for these women is more permissive than many assume. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists' Committee Opinion 659 says non-hormonal options should be the first choice, but that low-dose vaginal estrogen may be considered for women who do not respond to them, in consultation with their oncologist, and that the data do not show an increased risk of cancer recurrence with vaginal estrogen [9]. The 2020 Menopause Society statement takes a similar view, with extra caution for women on aromatase inhibitors [1].

Second, there are already studied non-estrogen choices: vaginal DHEA, ospemifene, and a disciplined routine of moisturizers and lubricants [1][3][4]. Pelvic floor physical therapy helps when pain comes partly from tight muscles. Oxytocin gel would be competing with these, not filling an empty space.

Other women who ask about non-estrogen options include those with a history of blood clots or who simply prefer to avoid hormones. For them, the same answer applies: the studied alternatives exist today.

## The honest conclusion

If you have dryness or pain with sex after menopause, the treatment you want exists and is not oxytocin. Low-dose vaginal estrogen or vaginal DHEA, with moisturizers and lubricants for milder symptoms, is what the evidence supports and what specialists recommend [1].

Oxytocin gel is a plausible idea with a real signal on exam findings and a stalled Phase 3. It has not been shown to fix the symptom that brings women to the doctor, and it is not approved [7][8]. If the Oxagon trial ever enrolls and reports a clear win on pain with sex, that would change this page. Given that the trial had not started at its last update, a result before 2028 looks unlikely.

If you have had breast cancer or have another reason to avoid estrogen, do not assume you are out of options. Ask a clinician trained in menopause care about DHEA, ospemifene, moisturizers and, in consultation with your oncologist, whether low-dose vaginal estrogen is reasonable for you [9].

And be wary of any clinic selling compounded "oxytocin cream" for menopause. It is not the gel from the trials, it has not been tested for this, and no one has shown it works. Talk to a doctor who knows your history first.
