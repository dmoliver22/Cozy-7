Warm touch from a partner, a loved one's voice, orgasm, breastfeeding, gazing at your dog and singing with other people all raise oxytocin in human studies. Those are the ones with measurements behind them. Warmth and "social connection" in general probably do too, but the human data is thinner than the internet suggests. Two honest caveats run through everything below: the effects are modest, and the way oxytocin is measured in most of these studies is shaky. The behaviors are still worth doing. What they are not is a drug, and the one trial that compared a spray to touch found the touch was doing the work.

## First, how to read any "raises oxytocin" claim

Almost every study on this page measured oxytocin in blood, saliva or urine. That is a problem, for three reasons.

The common commercial lab kits disagree with each other. Two widely used ways of preparing a blood sample give results that do not correlate at all, and different kits produce values that differ by two orders of magnitude on the same samples [1]. A 2013 methods review concluded that the kits favored in behavioral research since 2004 are unreliable on unprocessed human samples and tag molecules other than oxytocin [2].

A single blood level does not tell you what is happening in the brain, where the behavioral effects are supposed to occur. The 2025 Endocrine Reviews paper on oxytocin deficiency states that single blood measurements are unreliable and that no validated routine clinical test exists [3].

And oxytocin in blood rises and falls in minutes, so when you sample matters enormously.

So the honest translation of "X raised oxytocin" is "X probably raised oxytocin, by an amount nobody can measure well." That does not make the behaviors useless. The studies also measured things we can trust, like blood pressure, cortisol and how fast wounds heal. Where those moved too, pay attention.

## Touch and massage

This is the best-studied route and the one with the clearest downstream effects.

In a 2008 trial, 34 married couples were randomized to a four-week "warm touch" program or to a monitoring-only control. The program taught "listening touch" and neck-and-shoulder massage, which couples practiced for 30 minutes, three times a week. Salivary oxytocin was higher in the touch group both early and late in the program, the stress enzyme alpha-amylase was lower afterward, and husbands in the touch group had significantly lower 24-hour systolic blood pressure at the end [4].

Thirty-four couples is a small trial. But blood pressure is not a flaky measurement, and it moved.

A 2015 review by the researcher who has studied this longest lays out the mechanism: gentle, non-painful stimulation of the skin, including stroking, warmth and light pressure, activates sensory nerves that drive oxytocin release in the brain, which in turn lowers stress hormones and blood pressure [5]. Much of the detailed mechanistic work is in animals; the human trials, like the one above, measure the outcomes rather than the brain.

What it means for you: regular, unhurried, affectionate touch with someone you trust is the single best-supported thing on this list. Fifteen minutes of shoulder rubs a few times a week was enough to change blood pressure in that trial. It does not need to be sexual.

## Hugging and a loved one's voice

Hugs are usually folded into touch studies rather than tested alone. The most interesting hug study is actually a voice study.

Researchers put 61 girls aged 7 to 12 through a standardized stress test, giving a speech and doing math in front of strangers. Afterward, 19 were comforted in person by their mothers with physical contact, 20 spoke to their mothers on the phone, and 22 watched a neutral film. The girls who got the full in-person comfort showed the highest urinary oxytocin and the fastest drop in cortisol. The girls who only heard their mother's voice showed a strikingly similar profile. The film group got neither [6].

The finding has stuck because it was unexpected. A voice alone, with no touch, did most of what a hug did. If you are far from the people you love, the phone call is not a consolation prize.

## Sex and orgasm

Oxytocin's core jobs in the body are contracting the uterus and releasing milk, and both involve the same reflex that fires during sex.

The classic measurement was done in 1987. Nine men and 13 women had blood drawn before, during and after self-stimulation to orgasm. Oxytocin rose significantly from baseline to orgasm in men and in women, and in women who had more than one orgasm it rose further with the second [7]. The absolute changes were small in pg/mL terms and the samples were tiny.

The reverse experiment has also been run: give couples oxytocin before sex and see what changes. In 29 couples, intranasal oxytocin before intercourse produced modest effects on orgasm intensity and contentment and no change on most measures [8]. Sex raises oxytocin reliably. Adding oxytocin to sex does little.

## Breastfeeding

This one is not in doubt. Suckling triggers oxytocin release from the pituitary, which contracts the cells around the milk ducts and lets milk down. It is the hormone's defining function and the reason the only nasal oxytocin product ever approved in the US, back in 1960, was for nursing mothers who had trouble with let-down [5].

Nursing mothers also report calm during feeds, and the same review describes the anti-stress effects that come with repeated oxytocin release in lactation [5]. If you are breastfeeding, you are already doing the most powerful natural oxytocin stimulus there is. You do not need a product.

## Eye contact with your dog

A 2015 study in Science measured urinary oxytocin in dogs and their owners before and after 30 minutes together. Owners whose dogs gazed at them for longer showed a rise in oxytocin; so did the dogs. Owners of hand-raised wolves, which do not hold human gaze the same way, showed no such rise [9].

Thirty minutes with your dog, especially the kind of quiet mutual attention the study measured, is a legitimate entry on this list. There is no equivalent study for cats that we could find.

## Music and singing

A 2014 German study followed 21 amateur choir singers through two rehearsals, taking saliva at the start and 30 minutes in. On one occasion they sang; on the other they chatted. Oxytocin and reported well-being rose more with singing than with talking [10].

Twenty-one people, one study. We would call singing "plausible, with early evidence" rather than established. Listening to music alone has weaker data than making it with other people.

## Warmth

Warmth is widely listed as an oxytocin booster, and the mechanistic case is real: in the 2015 review, warm temperature on the skin is one of the non-painful sensory inputs that drive oxytocin release [5]. But most of that evidence is in animals. We could not find a human study that took people, warmed them up, and measured oxytocin. A warm bath may well help you relax. Whether it does so through oxytocin is a guess.

## Social connection

"Social connection raises oxytocin" is true in the sense that every item above is a form of it. As a standalone claim it is hard to test, because "connection" is not an intervention you can randomize. The strongest human evidence is the voice study: contact with a trusted person after stress, even by phone, moved oxytocin and cortisol [6]. Beyond that, be suspicious of lists that claim meditation, volunteering or gratitude journaling "boost oxytocin." They may be good for you. Nobody has shown the hormone is why.

## The trial that put it together

Here is the study that ties this page to everything else on this site.

In a randomized trial published in JAMA Psychiatry, 80 healthy couples each received four small suction-blister wounds on the forearm, then were assigned to daily intranasal oxytocin or placebo, with or without instructions to engage in positive interaction and affectionate touch at home. The outcome was how fast the wounds healed, measured objectively.

Oxytocin alone did not speed healing or lower cortisol. Oxytocin combined with affectionate touch, and less robustly with sexual activity, was associated with faster healing [11].

Read that carefully. The spray by itself did nothing measurable. The spray plus touch did. The touch was the active ingredient, with the drug possibly amplifying it. This is the same lesson as the 2009 couples study, where a single dose before a lab argument improved communication [12], and the 2025 trial of 96 couples in therapy, where the spray added nothing to the therapy [13]. Oxytocin seems to do its work, when it does any, inside a warm interaction. Without the interaction, there is nothing to amplify.

## What this means for you

If you came here wanting to feel closer to someone, the evidence points at the behaviors, not the hormone.

The things with the best human data are regular affectionate touch with a trusted partner, hearing the voice of someone who loves you when you are stressed, sex, breastfeeding if you are nursing, quiet time with your dog, and singing with others. The effects on oxytocin are real but modest and poorly measured. The effects on blood pressure, stress hormones and healing, in the trials that measured them, were real too.

If the problem is that touch, warmth and eye contact have drained out of your relationship, then no behavior on this list is easy, and that is the actual problem. The one trial that gave couples a hormone alongside therapy found the therapy helped and the hormone did not. Couples therapy, in person or online, is the thing with evidence for getting the touch back. The oxytocin will follow it. It never led.
