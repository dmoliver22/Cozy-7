Bremelanotide and oxytocin are both peptides, both sold by peptide clinics for sex, and often sold together in the same lozenge. That is where the similarity ends. Bremelanotide (research name PT-141, brand name Vyleesi) is FDA-approved for premenopausal women with low sexual desire, after two 24-week trials in 1,247 women [1][2]. Oxytocin is approved only as an injection for labor and postpartum bleeding, and it is not approved for any sexual use [4]. Its entire evidence base for sex is 88 people across two small studies, and in the one trial of women who actually had a sexual problem, it tied with placebo [5][6].

## Two different switches

The two drugs act on different receptors, which is the plain-English reason they do different things.

Bremelanotide activates melanocortin receptors, mainly one called MC4R [3]. These receptors sit in brain circuits that handle appetite, energy and sexual motivation. Bremelanotide came out of research on a tanning peptide; the melanocortin system also controls skin pigment, which is why one of its side effects is darkening of the skin [3]. It is given as a shot under the skin of the thigh or belly from a single-use autoinjector, used before sex rather than daily [1].

Oxytocin activates the oxytocin receptor, OXTR. In the body, that receptor is on the uterus and the milk ducts, which is why oxytocin is a labor drug [4]. In the brain, oxytocin receptors are involved in bonding, stress and social behavior, and that is the basis for selling it as an intimacy spray [8]. Whether a nasal spray gets enough oxytocin into the human brain to matter is still debated. A 2021 review of the whole field concluded the evidence was limited [7].

So one drug targets a desire circuit directly. The other targets a bonding hormone receptor and hopes the spray reaches it.

## Approval status: one yes, one no

Bremelanotide was approved on 21 June 2019 for premenopausal women with acquired, generalized hypoactive sexual desire disorder. That means low desire that developed after a period of normal desire, that is not limited to one partner or situation, and that causes real distress [1][2]. It is not approved for men, for postmenopausal women, or for arousal or orgasm problems [1].

Oxytocin's approved uses are inducing or strengthening labor and controlling bleeding after birth, as an injection [4]. No nasal spray, troche or cream containing oxytocin is FDA-approved for anything. Every oxytocin product sold for intimacy in the United States is compounded by a pharmacy under a prescription and used off-label.

When a clinic sells "PT-141" as a nasal spray or a troche, that is also a compounded product, not Vyleesi. The approved drug is the autoinjector, made by the approved manufacturer.

## What the trials found

### Bremelanotide: 1,247 women, two trials

The FDA based its approval on two identical 24-week, double-blind, placebo-controlled trials. Together they enrolled 1,247 premenopausal women with hypoactive sexual desire disorder [1][2].

The main measure was a desire score from a standard questionnaire. About 25 percent of women on bremelanotide had a meaningful improvement in that score (defined as a rise of 1.2 points or more), compared with 17 percent on placebo [2]. Distress about low desire also fell more in the drug group [1]. The number of satisfying sexual events per month, a secondary measure, did not differ significantly from placebo [3].

Plain-English read: a real but modest effect. For every 100 women who use it, roughly 8 more respond than would have on placebo. Most women on the drug did not have a meaningful change in desire.

### Oxytocin: 88 people, two studies

The first study, from Freiburg in 2014, gave 29 healthy couples oxytocin or placebo nasal spray to use at home before sex. Oxytocin did not change desire, arousal, erection or lubrication. It slightly raised how intense orgasm felt and how content people felt afterward, more so in men [5].

The second, from Vienna in 2015, enrolled 30 women aged 41 to 65 with diagnosed sexual dysfunction. Over several months, oxytocin spray improved their sexual function scores. So did placebo, by about the same amount [6]. The researchers concluded that paying attention to sex and talking about it with a partner was doing the work.

Plain-English read: no trial has shown oxytocin beats placebo for low desire. The only test in women with the actual problem was negative, and nothing has been replicated.

## Side effects

Here the picture flips. Bremelanotide has a longer and better-documented side-effect list, because it was studied properly.

In the trials, nausea affected about 40 percent of women, most often after the first injection, and flushing affected about 20 percent. Injection-site reactions (13 percent) and headache (11 percent) were next [3]. Bremelanotide also causes a temporary rise in blood pressure and slowing of the heart rate after each dose, so it is not for women with uncontrolled high blood pressure or known heart disease [3]. About 1 percent of women developed focal hyperpigmentation, darkened patches on the face, gums or breasts, which did not always fade after stopping [3].

Intranasal oxytocin has been well tolerated in trials, with nasal irritation and a reduced sense of smell among the common complaints. The approved injection carries warnings about water retention and low sodium at high doses, because oxytocin is a close chemical cousin of the hormone that controls water balance [4]. For a compounded spray used off-label for months, nobody has collected long-term safety data, so a short side-effect list partly reflects a short list of studies.

## The stacked troche

Some telehealth platforms sell oxytocin, bremelanotide and tadalafil in a single dissolving lozenge marketed for "connection and arousal" [14]. Tadalafil is an approved erection drug. Bremelanotide has the trials above. Oxytocin has the 88 people.

If you take all three and notice something, you have no way to know what did it, and you are paying a monthly price for the whole bundle. Note also that a compounded bremelanotide lozenge has not been tested for how much drug it delivers through the cheek, so the trial numbers for the injection do not transfer to it.

The FDA sent roughly 130 warning letters to telehealth companies between September 2025 and March 2026 over claims made about compounded drugs, including calling them "clinically proven" or "the same active ingredient" as an approved product [15]. A troche page that leans on Vyleesi's trial results is making exactly that kind of claim.

## What each costs

Vyleesi is expensive and almost never covered by insurance. A cash-price guide lists a carton of four autoinjectors at around $1,018 [12]. That is roughly $250 per use.

Compounded oxytocin nasal spray through telehealth runs about $99 to $206 a month depending on the company and plan [13]. Compounded "PT-141" and bundled troches are priced in the same range, and some platforms list the three-drug troche for under $100 a month [14].

The price gap tells you something. The approved drug with trial evidence costs five to ten times more per month than the compounded products with little or none, which is one reason patients drift toward the cheaper option even when the evidence points the other way.

## Why Vyleesi struggled

This is context, not a recommendation. A national launch in September 2019 by Palatin's partner AMAG lasted about a year. In July 2020 AMAG handed the rights back and paid $12.0 million at closing plus a further $4.3 million to exit the deal [9].

Gross sales afterward were small: about $809,000 in the quarter ending 30 September 2020, $944,000 the following quarter, and $1.78 million in the first quarter of 2021 [10]. In December 2023 Palatin sold the product to Cosette Pharmaceuticals for $12 million upfront, with up to $159 million more only if sales milestones are ever hit [11].

The reasons are not mysterious. The effect is modest, nausea is common, the drug is a shot, the price is high and insurers would not pay. A drug can pass the FDA's test and still fail the patient's test of whether it is worth the hassle. That is worth remembering when a clinic sells you the compounded version with none of the trial data and all of the side effects.

## Who should talk to a clinician about which

If you are a premenopausal woman whose desire dropped, is low in every situation, and bothers you, bremelanotide is one of two approved options to raise with a doctor, along with the daily pill flibanserin. Ask about the response rate, the nausea, the blood pressure effect and the price before deciding. Rule out the common causes first: a medication (antidepressants and hormonal birth control are frequent culprits), thyroid problems, depression, pain with sex, sleep and the relationship itself.

If you are a man, postmenopausal, or your problem is arousal or orgasm rather than desire, neither drug is approved for you, and a clinician who takes sexual medicine seriously is the right next step.

If you are considering oxytocin for sex, know what you are buying: a compounded product that has never beaten placebo in anyone with a sexual problem, sold on a bonding story that its own trials did not confirm [5][6]. You also cannot find out whether your oxytocin is "low"; no validated clinical test exists [8].

Talk to a doctor who knows your history. The right question is not "which peptide," but "what is actually causing this," and that question has an answer far more often than a spray does.
