Probably some of it does, and nobody knows how much. In rats, mice and monkeys, oxytocin sprayed into the nose shows up in brain regions like the amygdala and hippocampus and raises levels in spinal fluid [1]. In humans, the direct evidence is one small study of 15 people, and the leading review of the field concludes that "the extent to which intranasally administered oxytocin reaches the human brain remains uncertain" [1]. On top of that sits a second problem that gets less attention: trials have used anywhere from 6 to 160 IU a day, with no agreed dose and signs that more is not better. Anyone who tells you to "take X IU" is skipping over both problems.

## The nose-to-brain hypothesis

The brain is protected by the blood-brain barrier, a lining of tightly packed cells that keeps most large molecules out. Oxytocin is a peptide of nine amino acids. Injected into a vein, very little of it crosses that barrier.

The nose offers a possible back door. Two sets of nerves pass directly from the upper nasal cavity through the skull base into the brain: the olfactory nerves that carry smell, and branches of the trigeminal nerve that carry sensation from the face. A molecule deposited on the mucous membrane near those nerves can, in principle, travel along or between the nerve fibers into the brain and the fluid around it, bypassing the blood entirely [1].

That is the hypothesis. It is reasonable. The question is whether enough oxytocin takes that route, in people, with the devices and doses actually used, to change anything.

## What animal studies showed

The animal data is the strongest part of the case. Studies in rodents and non-human primates have tracked labeled oxytocin from the nose into the amygdala and hippocampus and shown functionally relevant increases in cerebrospinal fluid, the fluid that bathes the brain and spinal cord [1]. These studies can do things human studies cannot: sacrifice the animal, slice the brain, and measure directly.

They also have limits. Animal noses are built differently, with a far larger share of the nasal lining devoted to smell. Doses per kilogram are often much higher than any human receives. And an increase in spinal fluid is not the same as an increase at the receptors that matter for behavior.

## The human evidence and its limits

The one direct human measurement comes from a 2013 German study. Eleven volunteers received 24 IU of nasal oxytocin and four received placebo, and the researchers sampled both blood and cerebrospinal fluid afterward. Blood oxytocin peaked at 15 minutes and was falling by 75 minutes. Spinal fluid oxytocin took up to 75 minutes to rise significantly. The two did not correlate [2].

That pattern fits a direct nose-to-brain route: if the spinal fluid rise were just spillover from the blood, the two would track each other. But 11 people is 11 people. The study has not been repeated at scale, and a spinal tap is not something you can do routinely in healthy volunteers.

The indirect evidence is suggestive in both directions. A 2017 crossover study in 17 men with autism used a breath-powered device that deposits spray deep in the nose. An 8 IU dose changed performance on an emotion task even though blood oxytocin did not rise, which the authors read as a central effect [3]. A 24 IU dose with the same device did not reach significance. So one of the better delivery studies found an effect at the lower dose and not the higher one, in 17 people.

Blood levels, meanwhile, are easy to measure and not very informative about the brain. After a nasal dose, blood oxytocin rises within about 30 minutes and returns to baseline by around 90 minutes in some people, while staying elevated at 150 minutes in others [4]. That variability between people is itself a clue: the same spray does not deliver the same amount to everyone.

Here is the honest summary from the field's own methodologists in 2021: the human evidence that nasal oxytocin reaches the brain is limited, and this uncertainty is one of several reasons the field's early positive findings have been hard to replicate [1].

## The dose-equivalence problem

Even if delivery were settled, dose would not be.

Look at what trials have used. The MGH FOCUS study compares single doses of 6 IU and 24 IU [5]. The couples therapy trial used 40 IU before each session [6]. The 2026 alcohol trial gave 35 IU twice daily, 70 IU a day [7]. An opioid withdrawal study gave 80 IU twice daily, 160 IU a day [8]. The terminated tinnitus trial was designed around high doses sprayed four times a day [9].

That is a 27-fold range, and nobody has run the study that would say which end is right for which purpose.

Three things make it worse.

**Device.** A standard pump spray deposits most of its volume in the front of the nose, far from the olfactory region. Breath-powered and precision devices aim higher [3]. The same IU through two devices is not the same dose to the brain.

**Deposition.** Head position, sniffing, a cold, a deviated septum, and how hard you press the pump all change where the liquid lands. Trial staff coach participants on technique. A person at home does not get coached.

**Formulation.** Compounded sprays come at 50 IU/mL or 100 IU/mL [10]. A metered pump delivers a fixed volume, so the concentration decides how much drug each spray carries. Trials used specific pharmaceutical formulations with their own preservatives and pH. A compounded spray is a different product, and its stability over weeks in your bathroom is a question for the pharmacy, not the trial.

So when a telehealth page says the strength is "set by your provider," understand that the provider is choosing a number inside a 27-fold range that the research has not resolved, for a device and formulation the research did not use.

## Why the dose curve may not be a straight line

Several lines of evidence suggest that for oxytocin, more is not simply more.

In the 2017 device study, 8 IU did something and 24 IU did less [3]. A meta-analysis of schizophrenia trials found a possible effect only above 40 to 80 IU, but the signal vanished when one outlying study was removed [11]. Animal work describes "paradoxical high-dose inhibition," where high concentrations of oxytocin start to act on related receptors and blunt their own effect.

If the true curve is bell-shaped, a fixed "take X IU" instruction can land you below the window, inside it, or past it, with no way to tell which. It also means a bigger bottle is not a better bottle.

## Why Biohaven and Tonix are reformulating

Two public companies are now spending money on the delivery and dose problems, which tells you the problems are real.

Tonix Pharmaceuticals adds magnesium to its intranasal oxytocin (TNX-1900 and TNX-2900). The company's stated rationale is that magnesium potentiates oxytocin at its receptor and reduces the paradoxical high-dose inhibition, so that in animal models of head pain the pain relief increases with dose instead of falling off [12][13]. Its Prader-Willi program is planned for Phase 2 in early 2027, and the migraine program dosed its first participant in March 2026 [13].

Biohaven lists BHV-1955, described in its filings as "a novel, long-acting intranasal formulation of oxytocin" for tinnitus, in the preclinical part of its pipeline [14]. "Long-acting" is the point. Plain oxytocin has a half-life in blood of roughly half an hour, and the terminated NYU tinnitus trial had to spray four times a day to try to maintain any effect [9]. A formulation that lasts longer could make a once-daily dose plausible.

Neither program has human efficacy data yet. But both exist because plain oxytocin spray, the kind sold by compounding pharmacies today, has an inconsistent dose-response and a short duration. The companies are not reformulating a product that works fine.

## Why "take X IU" oversimplifies

Put it all together:

- You do not know what fraction of a nasal dose reaches your brain. The field does not either [1].
- You do not know whether your device and technique put the spray where it needs to go.
- You do not know whether the dose you have been given is below, inside, or above a window that may be bell-shaped.
- You do not know whether your compounded formulation behaves like the pharmaceutical one in the studies.
- And the studies themselves disagree with each other by a factor of 27.

A number on a prescription creates a feeling of precision that the science does not support. That is not a reason to assume the spray does nothing. It is a reason to be skeptical of anyone, seller or forum, who speaks about oxytocin dosing with confidence.

## Where this leaves you

The nose-to-brain route is plausible and shown in animals. In humans it rests on one 15-person study and inference. The dose question is less settled still. If you are using or considering a compounded spray, this is the context for every claim you read about it: the people selling it have not solved a problem that two drug companies are spending years on. When the FOCUS, pain and reformulation studies read out over the next two years, we will update this page. Until then, the honest answer to the question in the title is: probably some, and nobody can tell you how much.
