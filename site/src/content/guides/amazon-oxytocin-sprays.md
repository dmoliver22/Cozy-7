None of them contain oxytocin. Oxytocin is a prescription drug in the United States in every form, approved only as an injection for labor and postpartum bleeding and available otherwise only from a compounding pharmacy on a prescription [1][2][3]. A spray that any adult can order online without seeing a clinician cannot legally contain it, and the one that was ever tested did not. What the listings actually sell are pheromone sprays, homeopathic dilutions, and supplement "boosters." This page explains each, shows you how to read a label, and tells you what we are doing about it.

## Why real oxytocin cannot be sold over the counter

The approved oxytocin product carries the words "Rx only" on its label [1]. There is no over-the-counter oxytocin in the US, a fact even the telehealth companies that sell the prescription version state plainly on their own pages [2]. The compounded nasal sprays that do exist must be made by a licensed pharmacy for a named patient with a valid prescription [3].

That leaves two ways a non-prescription product could contain real oxytocin. Either it is being sold illegally, or it is being sold as a "research chemical" with a label saying it is not for people. The FDA has spent 2025 and 2026 shutting down the second category, sending more than 50 warning letters to "research use only" peptide sellers in September 2025 alone and treating the disclaimer as a cover story when the marketing is clearly aimed at human use [4]. We do not link to those sellers and we recommend you do not buy from them.

So if a marketplace listing ships to anyone, has no prescription step, and is not an obvious research-chemical vendor, you already know what it is not.

## What the listed products actually are

Search "oxytocin" on a general marketplace and you get three kinds of product. None of them is the hormone.

**Pheromone sprays.** These are colognes. The idea is that molecules in the spray are detected unconsciously by people near you and make them feel more trusting or attracted. They often name oxytocin in the title or the copy, sometimes alongside a claim that the spray "contains" or "triggers" it. Whatever is in the bottle, it is applied to your skin or clothes, not your nose, and it does not enter your body in any meaningful amount. It cannot raise your oxytocin.

**Homeopathic "oxytocin."** Homeopathic products are made by diluting a substance repeatedly, often to the point where, by the maker's own arithmetic, no molecule of the original remains. A homeopathic oxytocin product may have started with oxytocin. By the time it is bottled, it is water or sugar. Homeopathic drugs are not evaluated by the FDA for effectiveness, and the label will usually say so in small print.

**"Oxytocin booster" capsules and drops.** These are dietary supplements. The pitch is that some mix of vitamins, minerals, herbs or amino acids helps your body make more of its own oxytocin. We could find no human trial showing that any supplement does this. Supplements do not need FDA approval before sale, and their claims are not checked before they appear.

## The Liquid Trust finding

The best-known "oxytocin spray" was called Liquid Trust, sold by a company called Vero Labs, and marketed as an odorless spray that would create a "trusting atmosphere" around the wearer [5][6]. For years it was the product people meant when they said they had bought oxytocin online.

In 2013, a University of Washington researcher had it tested. The result was negative for oxytocin. The sample was described as "consistent with tap water" [7]. Reporting at the time noted that the product was being sold on major marketplaces alongside other "oxytocin" products [5].

That is the only independent lab result on a consumer "oxytocin" product that we have been able to find, and it is more than a decade old. The products on sale today have different names and the same claims.

## What the review pattern tells you

We read the reviews on the current best-selling "oxytocin" products. We did not count them, so treat this as an observation and not a statistic.

The pattern is a split. One cluster of reviewers reports a dramatic change, often within minutes: people were friendlier, a date went well, a spouse was warmer. Another cluster is angry: "not oxytocin," "fraud," "falsely advertised." There is very little in the middle.

Here is why that pattern matters. In controlled trials, real intranasal oxytocin produced no detectable subjective change. A review of 38 trials found participants could not tell whether they had received oxytocin or placebo [8]. Real oxytocin, at research doses, does not feel like anything. A product that produces a strong, noticeable effect within minutes is not behaving like oxytocin. It is behaving like expectation.

The angry reviews are usually from people who checked the ingredient list.

## How to read a label

You do not need a lab to rule most of these out. Look for:

- **A prescription step.** If there was none, there is no oxytocin. Full stop.
- **The active ingredient in international units.** Real oxytocin is measured in IU, or occasionally in micrograms. A label that says "proprietary blend," lists only botanicals, or gives a homeopathic potency like "6X" or "30C" is not an oxytocin product.
- **The word "pheromone."** That is a cologne, whatever else the title says.
- **A named manufacturer, lot number and expiration date.** Legitimate drug products have all three.
- **A storage instruction.** Oxytocin is heat-sensitive. A real oxytocin product tells you to refrigerate it. A pheromone spray does not need to.
- **Claims the FDA has specifically objected to.** "Clinically proven," "FDA approved," and "same active ingredient as" are the phrases that drew roughly 130 FDA warning letters to telehealth companies in 2025 and 2026 [9]. On a supplement or cologne listing, they mean even less.

One more caution applies to the research-chemical vendors we do not link to. In August 2026 the owner of one such company was sentenced to 70 months in prison after selling products with forged purity certificates, some contaminated with testosterone [10]. A certificate of analysis on a seller's website proves nothing unless you know who ran the test.

## The marketplace problem is not new

Large online marketplaces have struggled with this category for years. In 2020, an investigation found 66 listings for injectable peptides on one major marketplace despite a promised crackdown, with listings removed and then reappearing [11]. In December 2025, reporting found unapproved peptides and performance-enhancing drugs still being sold by large retailers by mail [12]. Oxytocin-branded products sit in the same grey zone: not quite illegal to list as a cologne or supplement, impossible to police as a drug claim.

## Our independent lab test

We think the right answer to "what is in these bottles" is to find out.

The Oxytocin Report plans to purchase the best-selling consumer products sold under the name "oxytocin" on major US marketplaces and have them independently tested for oxytocin content. We will not use the cheap antibody-based kits that the research literature has shown produce results varying across two orders of magnitude between methods [13]. We will use a mass-spectrometry method, the same type of assay used by academic core laboratories to measure oxytocin in human samples [14], at a laboratory with no connection to any seller.

We will publish the product names, the lab, the method, the detection limit, and every result, including any that surprise us. We have no results yet, and we will not speculate about them here. When they are in, this page will be updated and we will publish a separate report.

## Where this leaves you

If you bought one of these sprays, you did not buy oxytocin, and you were not alone. The sellers rely on a word that sounds like medicine attached to a product that is legally a cologne or a supplement. Real oxytocin requires a prescription, has a thin evidence base for the things these products promise, and does not feel like anything when it works. If what you were hoping for was more closeness, more trust, or a better relationship, the things with actual evidence are not in a bottle, and we have written about them on our page on raising oxytocin naturally.
