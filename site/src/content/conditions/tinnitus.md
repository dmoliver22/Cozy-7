## The short answer

Probably not, and nobody knows for sure. One small pilot in Brazil in 2017 found that an oxytocin nasal spray reduced tinnitus in 15 people who knew they were getting it, and again in 16 people who were switched between oxytocin and placebo [1]. That is the entire published human evidence.

The one US trial that tried to confirm it, at NYU Langone, was terminated when the lead investigator left [2]. A drug company now owns a long-acting version of the idea, but it has not been given to a single human yet [5].

If you have tinnitus today, oxytocin is a research question, not a treatment.

## Why people hope oxytocin will help

Tinnitus is sound with no outside source: ringing, hissing, buzzing. For most people it comes with some hearing loss. The ear starts it, but the brain keeps it going, and the parts of the brain that decide how distressing the sound is matter as much as the sound itself.

That is where oxytocin comes in. Oxytocin receptors are dense in the amygdala and nearby limbic areas, the circuits that attach fear and urgency to a sensation [1]. The Brazilian team's idea was that quieting that distress network might make the ringing quieter, or at least easier to ignore [1].

The hope spread because tinnitus has no approved drug at all [9]. A 2020 news story about the NYU trial, a patient forum thread that runs past ten pages, and a 2026 investor presentation from a drug company have all kept the idea alive [3][8][6].

## What the research actually found

### The 2017 Brazilian pilot: 15 people, then 16

Azevedo and colleagues at the Federal University of São Paulo published two small studies in one paper in Frontiers in Neurology [1].

Study 1 was open-label: 15 patients with chronic tinnitus used oxytocin nasal spray for 10 weeks, and both patients and doctors knew what they were taking. Tinnitus scores fell on several scales [1].

Study 2 was stronger. Sixteen patients used oxytocin and a placebo spray in turn, without knowing which was which. Tinnitus sensation again dropped while on oxytocin [1]. The main yardsticks were questionnaires, including the Tinnitus Handicap Inventory, which scores how much the sound interferes with daily life, and a clinician's overall impression of change [1].

Plain-English read: this is a promising signal from 31 people in total, generated by one group, measured with questionnaires. The open-label part cannot separate drug from expectation. The crossover part can, but 16 people is far too few to rule out chance or a short-lived effect. The authors said so themselves and called for larger controlled trials [1].

### The NYU trial: terminated before it could answer

NYU Langone registered NCT04210310, "High Dose Oxytocin Nasal Spray for Treatment of Tinnitus," a placebo-controlled pilot led by Dr. Lawrence C. Newman. It started in January 2020 [2]. Press coverage described a plan for around 30 patients using oxytocin or placebo spray four times a day for six months [3]. The registry lists 33 people enrolled [2].

The trial reached its primary completion date in May 2022 and was then marked terminated, with the reason given as the investigator's departure from the institution [2]. Results were submitted to the registry in August 2022, but no peer-reviewed publication has appeared as of October 2026 [2].

Plain-English read: this was the study that could have confirmed or killed the Brazilian finding. It did neither. Whatever data exist have not been analyzed in public.

Two details of its design still matter. First, "high dose" meant four sprays a day for six months, far more exposure than the 10 weeks in Brazil [2][3]. Second, it was still a pilot, not a trial sized to prove benefit. Even a clean positive result would have called for a bigger study, not a prescription.

### The patent and the company

Newman and a co-inventor named Cramer hold US patent 11,241,477, "Oxytocin compositions for treatment of tinnitus." It was first assigned to NYU and later to a company called Maskbegone LLC [4]. In 2026 Biohaven, a publicly traded drug developer, acquired oxytocin derivatives for tinnitus through an agreement with Maskbegone, with an upfront payment in the first quarter of the year, according to its quarterly filing [7].

Biohaven calls the program BHV-1955, "a novel, long-acting intranasal formulation of oxytocin, for the central treatment of tinnitus" [7]. It sits in the preclinical section of the company's pipeline, meaning animal and laboratory work only [5]. Newman presented it at Biohaven's R&D Day on 27 May 2026 [6]. No application to begin human testing and no Phase 1 date have been announced [5][7].

Plain-English read: "long-acting" tells you what the company thinks the problem was. Oxytocin peaks in the blood within about 15 to 45 minutes and is half gone roughly 30 minutes later, which is why the NYU design needed four sprays a day [11]. A formulation that lasts longer is a bet on chemistry, not new evidence that oxytocin works.

### What patients report

On TinnitusTalk, a long-running tinnitus patient forum, a thread titled "The 'Love Hormone' (Oxytocin) May Quiet Tinnitus" runs to at least 10 pages [8]. The accounts there are reported but not independently verified.

One member got a family doctor to prescribe compounded nasal oxytocin, used it several times a day on a schedule similar to the NYU trial for more than a month, and described "little to no effect" [8]. Another bought product from overseas, felt warm and flushed with burning sinuses, and noticed no change in tinnitus [8]. One member believes oxytocin made their tinnitus worse [8].

Plain-English read: forum posts are not data, but they are the closest thing to real-world experience outside a trial, and they do not echo the Brazilian result.

### Does the spray even reach the brain?

Every tinnitus theory about oxytocin depends on it reaching the amygdala and related circuits. Animal studies show labeled oxytocin reaching those regions after nasal dosing, but a 2021 review concluded that how much reaches the human brain "remains uncertain" [11]. Trial doses across all conditions have ranged from 6 to 160 IU a day, which tells you the field has not settled on what a working amount would even be [11].

## What's being tested right now

Nothing in humans. As of October 2026 there is no registered oxytocin trial for tinnitus that is recruiting or active. The only one ever registered, NCT04210310, is terminated [2].

The one program with money behind it is Biohaven's BHV-1955, and it is preclinical [5]. For it to matter to you, several things have to happen in order: the company files to start human testing, a Phase 1 safety study runs in healthy volunteers, and then a placebo-controlled trial in people with tinnitus reads out. None of those steps has a public date [5][7]. A realistic guess is first human data no earlier than 2028, and that is a guess.

Watch for: a new NCT number pairing "oxytocin" with "tinnitus," a Biohaven announcement that it has filed to begin human studies, or publication of the NYU data. Any of those would be real news. Another forum thread would not.

One more thing would need to happen before any of this reaches a clinic. Someone has to show that the drug reaches the brain regions it is supposed to act on, in humans, at the amount used. That question is open for every nasal oxytocin product, not just this one [11].

## Where this leaves you

A careful person would conclude this: oxytocin for tinnitus rests on one 2017 pilot in 31 people, the confirmatory trial never finished, and the only path forward is a company program that has not reached humans [1][2][5]. That is weaker than it sounds on a forum, where the Brazilian paper has been circulating for nine years.

What would change the picture is simple. A placebo-controlled trial of at least a few hundred people, run by a group other than the original one, showing a meaningful drop in tinnitus distress. Until then, a prescription for compounded oxytocin spray is an experiment on yourself, with no approval behind it and at least one forum member's month of trying with nothing to show [8].

Meanwhile, tinnitus has real management options, even without a pill. No drug is approved for tinnitus [9], but a hearing evaluation, hearing aids when there is loss, cognitive behavioral therapy aimed at tinnitus distress, and sound therapy all have evidence behind them [9][10]. A 2020 Cochrane review found CBT may reduce how much tinnitus affects quality of life, with few or no side effects [10]. Those are worth your time this month. If you want to talk about oxytocin anyway, talk to a doctor who knows your history and ask what they would want to see before prescribing it.
