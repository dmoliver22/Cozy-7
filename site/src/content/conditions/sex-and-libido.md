## The short answer

Barely, and not in the way the ads suggest.

Two controlled studies have tested oxytocin for sex in humans.

In 29 healthy couples, a dose of nasal oxytocin before sex did not change desire, arousal, erection, or lubrication. It slightly raised how intense orgasm felt and how content people felt afterward, mostly in men [1].

In 30 women with sexual dysfunction, months of oxytocin spray improved their sex lives, and so did placebo spray, by about the same amount [2][3].

That is the entire evidence base. No trial has shown oxytocin beats placebo for low desire, trouble reaching orgasm, or arousal problems.

Two other drugs have passed that test for premenopausal women. Oxytocin has not been given the chance, and the one time it was, it tied with salt water.

## Why people hope oxytocin will help

Low desire, flat orgasms, or feeling nothing during sex is lonely and hard to talk about. Many people land here after a doctor shrugged.

The biology sounds right. Oxytocin surges at orgasm in both men and women. It is released during touch and nursing.

It is called the love hormone and the cuddle hormone in every magazine. If your body makes it during sex, more of it should mean better sex. That is the logic.

The marketing runs on that logic. Telehealth companies sell compounded oxytocin nasal spray for "intimacy" and "connection," typically used, in one seller's words, thirty to sixty minutes before shared time [4]. Observed prices in October 2026 ranged from about $99 to $206 a month [4][5].

One platform bundles oxytocin with bremelanotide and tadalafil in a single lozenge sold for "connection and arousal" [6]. Those two added ingredients have proven effects, which makes it impossible for a buyer to know what oxytocin is contributing. The FDA has sent roughly 130 warning letters to telehealth companies since September 2025 over exactly this kind of compounded-drug marketing [7].

Here is what the studies say it contributes.

## What the research actually found

### 2014: Oxytocin before sex in 29 couples

Behnia and colleagues at the University of Freiburg recruited 29 healthy heterosexual couples, 58 people in all, and gave each partner 24 IU of oxytocin or placebo nasal spray to use at home before sex. It was double-blind and placebo-controlled. After each encounter, partners filled out questionnaires about drive, arousal, orgasm, and how they felt about each other [1].

Oxytocin did not change the classic measures of sexual function: desire, arousal, erection, or lubrication. It did produce specific effects around orgasm and afterward.

People reported more intense orgasms and more contentment after sex, and said they felt more able to share sexual wishes and understand their partner. The effects were more pronounced in men [1].

Plain-English read: a small, real, and narrow effect in people who already had a healthy sex life. Nothing moved on desire or arousal, the two things most people searching this topic actually want to fix. The study has not been repeated.

### 2015: Oxytocin in women with sexual dysfunction (30 women)

This is the study that should end most sales pitches. Muin and colleagues at the Medical University of Vienna enrolled 30 women aged 41 to 65 with diagnosed sexual dysfunction, in relationships lasting 2 to 33 years. In a randomized, placebo-controlled design, the women were assigned to use either oxytocin nasal spray or placebo spray before sex over several months [2][3].

Oxytocin improved sexual function scores. So did placebo. The two were statistically indistinguishable [2].

The researchers' own conclusion was striking: sexual dysfunction in women is often less about a missing chemical and more about communication with a partner and everyday stress. Being in a study, paying attention to sex, and talking about it seemed to do the work [2].

Plain-English read: when oxytocin was tested against placebo in women who actually had the problem, it did not win. This is the only such trial, and it was negative.

### Case reports and the missing middle

Beyond these two studies, the literature is single-patient case reports and animal work. Clinics cite them. A case report cannot separate a drug effect from chance, expectation, or time, and we do not count them as evidence here.

There is also a basic open question underneath everything: whether nasal oxytocin reaches the human brain in meaningful amounts. A 2021 review concluded the evidence is still limited [10]. If the spray mostly stays in the nose and bloodstream, the brain-based "connection" story has a problem before it starts.

### How this compares with approved drugs

Two medications are FDA-approved for hypoactive sexual desire disorder, meaning persistently low desire that causes distress, in premenopausal women. Neither is oxytocin.

Bremelanotide, a self-injection taken before sex, was approved in June 2019 after two 24-week trials in 1,247 women. About a quarter of women on the drug had a meaningful rise in desire score, versus 17 percent on placebo. Nausea was the most common side effect [8].

Flibanserin, a daily pill, was approved in August 2015. In trials it added roughly half to one satisfying sexual event per month over placebo, and it carries a boxed warning about dangerously low blood pressure when combined with alcohol [9].

Both are approved only for premenopausal women. Neither is approved for men or for women after menopause, where low desire is at least as common. Those are modest effects, and we are not recommending either.

The point is the standard. Each went through placebo-controlled trials in more than a thousand women before anyone could sell it for desire. Oxytocin has been tested in 88 people total across both studies, and lost to placebo in the one group that had the problem.

## What's being tested right now

No registered trial is testing oxytocin for low libido or orgasm problems in the general population.

The closest thing is OxyPLEASURE, NCT06808516, at University Hospital Basel. It is a double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover trial of intranasal oxytocin on sexual well-being in 42 people. Half have arginine vasopressin deficiency, a pituitary condition in which a true oxytocin deficiency has been shown, and half are healthy controls.

Part A is a 7-day treatment in people in stable relationships; Part B is a single dose with measures of arousal, fear, and empathy. Primary completion is expected in December 2026 [11].

If OxyPLEASURE finds an effect, it will most likely be in the deficiency group. That would be important for people with pituitary disease. It would not tell a healthy 45-year-old with low desire anything about whether a spray will help her.

What would need to happen for the picture to change: a placebo-controlled trial of at least a few hundred people with a diagnosed sexual problem, run for months, measuring desire and distress. Nothing like that is registered.

There is a reason the bar is that high. Sexual function trials rely on self-report, and self-report moves a lot with attention and expectation. That is exactly what the Vienna study saw: both groups improved, because both groups were thinking and talking about sex for months.

A small trial cannot separate a drug from that effect. The two approved drugs each needed more than a thousand women to show a modest difference. Oxytocin would need the same.

## Where this leaves you

A careful person would conclude that oxytocin spray for sex is a story that outran its data. One small study in healthy couples found slightly more intense orgasms, mostly for men. The only trial in women with a real problem found placebo did just as well.

Nothing has been replicated. Nothing is approved.

The picture would change if OxyPLEASURE shows a clear effect in people with proven deficiency, or if someone runs a properly sized trial in the general population. Neither will arrive before 2027.

One more thing: you cannot find out if your oxytocin is low. No validated clinical test exists, single blood measurements are unreliable, and research assays disagree with each other by enormous margins [12]. Anyone selling a spray to fix a deficiency they cannot measure is guessing.

The things that do help with low desire and orgasm trouble are less glamorous and far better tested. A clinician who takes libido seriously will check hormones, review your medications, ask about pain, sleep, mood, and the relationship, and name the likely cause. Sex therapy works on the communication and pressure that the Vienna study suggested were doing the real damage.

And for premenopausal women with distressing low desire, two approved medications exist, with honest, modest numbers behind them. Start with the diagnosis, not the spray.
