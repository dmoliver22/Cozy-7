## The short answer

For alcohol, oxytocin has been tested properly and did not work. The largest trial, published in July 2026, gave 100 adults with alcohol use disorder up to 70 IU a day of intranasal oxytocin or placebo for 12 weeks. There was no difference in heavy drinking days [1].

A 2026 meta-analysis pooling the randomized trials reached the same conclusion: intranasal oxytocin does not confer a consistent benefit across alcohol-related outcomes [2]. Adding oxytocin to couples therapy for drinking did not help either, in a 96-couple trial [3].

For opioid and benzodiazepine withdrawal, the picture is different but thin. One 20-person study found a short-term signal, and one 60-person trial has finished without publishing [4][6]. That is early, not proven.

Approved medications for alcohol and opioid use disorder exist, work, and are underused. They are where to start.

## Why people hope oxytocin will help

Addiction isolates people. It damages relationships, raises stress, and makes social situations feel threatening. Oxytocin is the hormone of bonding and calm, so the hope is obvious: if you could restore connection and lower stress, maybe the craving would ease.

The biology gave that hope some backing. Oxytocin acts on the brain's stress and reward circuits, the same circuits alcohol and opioids hijack. In animals, oxytocin reduces alcohol intake and softens withdrawal.

Early human lab work showed oxytocin damped the brain's alarm response to upsetting images in people with alcohol use disorder [8].

There was also a social logic. Many people drink or use in response to conflict, loneliness or anger. If oxytocin makes people calmer and more trusting, perhaps it could help in therapy, especially couples therapy, where trust is the whole point.

That is a reasonable set of ideas. The trials below tested them.

## What the research actually found

### The 2026 multisite alcohol trial: 100 adults, 12 weeks, no effect on drinking

This is the definitive study so far. Funded by the National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism and led by Nassima Tiouririne and colleagues, it ran at four US sites: UCLA, Johns Hopkins, Boston Medical Center and the University of Virginia [1].

One hundred adults with alcohol use disorder were randomly assigned to intranasal oxytocin, 35 IU twice a day for a total of up to 70 IU daily, or a matching placebo, for 12 weeks. The main outcome was the percentage of heavy drinking days [1].

Published in Alcohol: Clinical and Experimental Research in July 2026, the result was null. Heavy drinking days did not differ between the oxytocin and placebo groups.

Two secondary findings stood out. The oxytocin group scored lower on anger and physical aggression measures. And the most common side effect was a reduced sense of smell, which the trial tracked with a standard scratch-and-sniff test [1].

Plain read: at a high daily dose, for three months, with 100 people, oxytocin did not change drinking. The anger and aggression finding is a lead for future research, not a treatment. The smell finding is a reminder that spraying a peptide into your nose twice a day for months is not nothing.

### The 2026 meta-analysis: no consistent benefit

A 2026 paper in Psychoneuroendocrinology pooled the randomized trials of intranasal oxytocin in alcohol use disorder using Bayesian and multilevel methods, which are designed to handle small, varied studies honestly [2].

The conclusion was that intranasal oxytocin "does not confer a consistent therapeutic benefit across alcohol-related outcomes." The authors did note what they called context-sensitive signals in social and cognitive measures, meaning oxytocin sometimes shifted how people processed social or emotional information, without that translating into less drinking [2].

Plain read: when you add up every controlled trial, the drinking outcomes do not move. This is why our verdict is "tested, didn't work" rather than "mixed."

### Oxytocin plus couples therapy: 96 couples, no added benefit

Because oxytocin's effects seem to depend on social context, researchers tested it in the most social treatment there is. Alcohol Behavioral Couple Therapy brings both partners into treatment for one partner's drinking [3].

In a randomized trial published in the Journal of Clinical Psychiatry in 2025, 96 couples got either 40 IU of intranasal oxytocin or placebo before therapy sessions, 49 on oxytocin and 47 on placebo [3].

Oxytocin was safe and tolerable. It added nothing. Drinking outcomes and relationship outcomes were no better with oxytocin than with therapy alone [3].

Plain read: even in the setting where oxytocin should shine, it did not beat the therapy by itself. The therapy, notably, helped on its own.

### Brain scans: a calmer alarm response, but no change in drinking

A 2025 randomized crossover study scanned men and women with alcohol use disorder after oxytocin and after placebo while they viewed upsetting images. Oxytocin blunted the response of the amygdala, the brain's threat detector [8].

Plain read: this is a mechanism finding. It shows oxytocin does something measurable in the brains of people with alcohol problems. It does not show that this change leads to less drinking, and the 12-week trial above suggests it does not [1][8].

### Opioid withdrawal: one small early study

The opioid evidence is a single Phase 1 study from Brown University, published in January 2025. Twenty people on opioid agonist therapy, such as buprenorphine or methadone, received 80 IU of intranasal oxytocin twice a day for 7 days or placebo [4].

In a lab session designed to trigger withdrawal-like feelings and anxiety through drug cues and a stress hormone challenge, oxytocin significantly reduced those cue-triggered symptoms [4].

Plain read: a real signal, in 20 people, for one week, measured in a lab. It has not been repeated, and nobody has tested whether it changes opioid use, retention in treatment or overdose. This is what "early signals" means.

### Benzodiazepine withdrawal: 60 people, finished, unpublished

St. Olavs Hospital in Norway ran a pilot randomized trial in 60 inpatients, aged 18 to 65, who were tapering off benzodiazepines at doses between 20 and 80 mg of diazepam equivalent.

Participants got 16 IU of oxytocin three times a day, 48 IU daily, or placebo for 21 days during the taper. Outcomes included withdrawal symptoms, sleep, anxiety and craving [6].

The trial is listed as completed as of 2026. No results have been published that we could find. Until they are, there is simply no evidence either way [6].

### A caveat on all of it

A 2021 review of the entire intranasal oxytocin field concluded that how much nasal oxytocin reaches the human brain remains uncertain [7]. Every study above assumes it reaches the circuits that matter. That assumption is not settled.

## What's being tested right now

Alcohol: we found no active, recruiting trial of intranasal oxytocin for alcohol use disorder in the United States after the 2026 multisite trial reported. Given its result and the meta-analysis, that is not surprising.

Opioids: NCT05761860 at the University of Florida is testing whether oxytocin changes pain relief, decision-making and abuse potential when given with oxycodone in older adults. It involves 6 lab sessions per participant. Primary completion is estimated for December 2026, with the full study wrapping in July 2027 [5].

Benzodiazepines: the Norwegian trial NCT06757517 has finished and a publication is the thing to watch for [6].

For a company sponsor, there is none. No pharmaceutical company is pursuing oxytocin for any addiction indication that we could find. The remaining work is academic.

## Where this leaves you

A careful person would conclude that oxytocin for alcohol is settled, at least for now. A 100-person, 12-week trial at a high dose found no effect on drinking [1]. A meta-analysis of the trials found no consistent benefit [2].

Adding it to couples therapy added nothing [3]. The small signals on anger, aggression and social processing are scientifically interesting and clinically unproven.

For opioid and benzodiazepine withdrawal, the honest statement is: one 20-person study, one unpublished 60-person trial, and one lab study underway [4][5][6]. Something could emerge, but nothing has yet.

What would change the picture: a replicated withdrawal result in a trial that measures actual drug use, or a published positive benzodiazepine result from Norway.

In the meantime, alcohol and opioid use disorders have treatments with strong evidence. Naltrexone and acamprosate reduce heavy drinking. Buprenorphine and methadone cut overdose deaths.

Behavioral therapy and peer support work alongside them, and the couples therapy in the trial above helped on its own. If you or someone you love is struggling, an addiction medicine clinician or a therapist who treats substance use can lay out those options. Talk to a doctor who knows your history before trying any compounded or off-label product.
