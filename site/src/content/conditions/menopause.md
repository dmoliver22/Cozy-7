## The short answer

Oxytocin has not been shown to fix menopause symptoms. The best-studied use is a vaginal gel for dryness and thinning of the vaginal lining, a condition doctors call vaginal atrophy or genitourinary syndrome of menopause.

A 2023 meta-analysis pooled 7 small trials with 631 women. When a clinician examined the tissue, atrophy looked better with oxytocin gel. But pain during sex, vaginal pH and tissue samples under the microscope did not differ from placebo [1].

For mood, sleep and that sense of feeling flat or disconnected, no completed trial in menopausal women shows a benefit from oxytocin.

Low-dose vaginal estrogen and vaginal DHEA are approved, studied in thousands of women, and are what menopause specialists reach for first [9]. Oxytocin gel is not approved anywhere we could find.

## Why people hope oxytocin will help

Menopause drops estrogen, and low estrogen thins and dries the vaginal lining. Sex can start to hurt, and many women also notice low mood, poor sleep and a feeling of being less connected to their partner.

Oxytocin enters the picture in two ways. First, vaginal and uterine tissue carry oxytocin receptors, and early lab and pilot work suggested oxytocin applied directly could encourage the lining to grow back [2]. That is the idea behind the gel.

Second, estrogen helps the body make oxytocin. So it is reasonable to ask whether falling estrogen also lowers oxytocin, and whether that explains some of the emotional flatness women describe. That question is now being tested in Basel [4].

The hype came later. In 2025, conference findings were covered under headlines saying the "love hormone" may ease menopause mood swings [7].

Menopause newsletters picked up the theme, and compounded oxytocin sprays are now marketed to midlife women for intimacy and connection. The marketing has run well ahead of the trials.

## What the research actually found

### The Swedish pilot: where the gel idea started

The first controlled test of oxytocin gel was a small Swedish pilot published in 2011. Postmenopausal women used either an oxytocin gel or a placebo gel in the vagina, with neither the women nor the researchers knowing who got which [2].

The researchers reported that signs of vaginal atrophy reversed in the oxytocin group. Plain read: a first look in a small number of women, exactly the kind of result that needs repeating before anyone acts on it.

### The 2023 meta-analysis: 7 trials, 631 women

By 2023 there were enough small trials to pool. The meta-analysis combined 7 randomized trials with 631 postmenopausal women, comparing vaginal oxytocin gel with placebo [1].

Three findings matter. Clinically assessed atrophy, meaning how the tissue looked and felt to the examining clinician, improved with oxytocin. Dyspareunia, the medical word for pain during sex, did not differ significantly from placebo.

Vaginal pH and histology, which is the tissue sample viewed under a microscope, also did not differ [1]. Those two measures matter because they are the objective markers doctors use to confirm that the lining has truly recovered. Estrogen moves both of them.

Plain read: the exam looked better, but the thing women most want fixed, pain with sex, did not clearly change. The trials were small, used different gel strengths and lengths of treatment, and followed women for short periods.

One more thing to keep in mind: a placebo gel is itself a moisturizer. Some of the improvement in both groups likely came from simply applying something soothing, which makes a small true oxytocin effect harder to see. That is why the honest verdict is "mixed," not "works" or "doesn't work."

The gel is applied to the vagina, so this evidence says nothing about nasal sprays, which deliver oxytocin to a different place for a different purpose.

### Mood, sleep and the "love hormone" angle

Here the evidence thins out fast. In 2025, press reports on a conference presentation said oxytocin "may ease mood swings during menopause and postpartum" [7]. We could not verify a published, peer-reviewed trial in menopausal women behind that coverage, so treat it as early research reported in the news, not a treatment result.

We found no completed randomized trial of intranasal oxytocin for hot flashes, sleep, mood or libido in menopausal women. None.

There is also a basic open question with nasal sprays. A 2021 review of the whole intranasal oxytocin field concluded that the extent to which nasal oxytocin reaches the human brain remains uncertain [8]. So even the mechanism behind the mood claims is unsettled.

### Does menopause actually lower oxytocin?

Nobody can say yet, and that is worth sitting with for a moment. Measuring oxytocin is hard. A 2025 review in Endocrine Reviews states that no validated routine clinical test exists and that single blood measurements are unreliable [6].

What has been shown is that true oxytocin deficiency exists in a specific group: people with arginine-vasopressin deficiency, a pituitary condition. In a 2023 Basel study, a stimulation test raised oxytocin sharply in healthy people but not in those patients [5]. That is a different population from women going through menopause.

The Basel group is now asking whether postmenopausal women also run lower. Their OxyMENO study, which started on 20 August 2026, measures neurophysin-I, a stand-in marker for oxytocin, over one month in postmenopausal and premenopausal women [4]. Until it reports, "menopause lowers your oxytocin" is a hypothesis, not a fact.

### How this compares with vaginal estrogen

This is the comparison that matters. The North American Menopause Society's 2020 position statement, based on decades of trials, estimates that genitourinary syndrome of menopause affects somewhere between 27% and 84% of postmenopausal women [9].

Its recommendations are clear. For milder symptoms, start with non-hormonal lubricants and long-acting vaginal moisturizers. For moderate to severe symptoms, the prescription options are low-dose vaginal estrogen, vaginal DHEA inserts and oral ospemifene [9].

These treatments improve dryness, pain with sex and vaginal pH, and they are approved by regulators. Low-dose vaginal estrogen in particular stays mostly in the local tissue, which is why specialists consider it suitable for most women, including many who have been told to avoid hormone pills [9].

Oxytocin gel, by contrast, has 7 small trials and did not clearly improve pain with sex or pH [1]. It is not approved for this use. If you have been told you cannot use estrogen, the studied alternatives are DHEA, ospemifene and moisturizers, not oxytocin.

## What's being tested right now

Two registered studies are directly relevant.

NCT06514586 is a Phase 3, double-blind, randomized trial of a vaginal oxytocin gel against placebo in postmenopausal women with vulvovaginal atrophy. It plans to enroll about 242 women [3].

A Phase 3 is the kind of trial regulators look at, so this is the one that could change the picture. The sponsor and expected completion date were not visible in the registry summary we could access.

NCT07742124, called OxyMENO, is run by University Hospital Basel. It started on 20 August 2026 and is recruiting. It does not treat anyone.

It measures cumulative neurophysin-I over one month in postmenopausal women and compares it with premenopausal controls, testing the hypothesis that levels are lower after menopause. Primary completion is estimated for June 2027, with the full study wrapping up around September 2027 [4].

We found no registered trial of intranasal oxytocin for menopausal mood, sleep or hot flashes.

## Where this leaves you

A careful person reading this today would conclude three things.

The vaginal gel is a real research idea with a real signal on exam findings, and a Phase 3 trial is the right next step. But it has not yet been shown to fix the symptom women care about most, pain with sex, and it is not an approved treatment [1][3].

The mood and connection claims are ahead of the evidence. There is no completed trial in menopausal women, and it is not yet known whether menopause lowers oxytocin at all [4][6].

Two things would change this picture: a positive Phase 3 readout on pain with sex, or an OxyMENO result showing postmenopausal women truly run low on oxytocin. Watch for both in 2027.

In the meantime, the treatments for vaginal dryness and painful sex are well established. Low-dose vaginal estrogen and vaginal DHEA work, moisturizers and lubricants help with milder symptoms, and pelvic floor physical therapy addresses the muscle side of pain.

A clinician trained in menopause care can walk you through them. Talk to a doctor who knows your history before trying anything compounded or off-label.
